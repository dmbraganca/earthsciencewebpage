Iceland

Fire, Ice, and a People Shaped by Both

Iceland sits in the North Atlantic, straddling the Mid-Atlantic Ridge at the boundary of the North American and Eurasian tectonic plates. It is one of the most geologically active places on Earth, yet it is also home to a stable, prosperous society that has found ways to live alongside forces that would overwhelm most nations. Few countries offer such a direct window into how the planet works.

Historical Background

Norse settlers arrived in Iceland around 874 AD, led by Ingólfr Arnarson. In 930 AD they established the Althing, a national assembly that is widely regarded as one of the world's oldest functioning parliaments. The island was later settled by Celtic peoples brought from the British Isles. This mixed Norse-Celtic heritage shaped the sagas — long prose narratives that remain among the great works of medieval literature.

Cultural Identity

Icelandic culture places a strong emphasis on storytelling, language preservation, and connection to the landscape. The Icelandic language has changed so little since the 12th century that modern speakers can read the original sagas without much difficulty. Folk beliefs in hidden people (huldufólk) — elves and spirits said to inhabit rocks and hills — remain a quietly persistent part of the national identity.

Society

Iceland consistently ranks near the top of global indexes for gender equality, press freedom, and quality of life. Its population of roughly 370,000 is small, which contributes to a tight-knit social fabric. Universal healthcare and strong public education are part of the national infrastructure. Iceland was also one of the first countries to legalize same-sex marriage, doing so in 2010.

Economy

Fishing long dominated Iceland's economy, and the country fought the so-called "Cod Wars" with Britain in the 20th century to protect its fishing grounds. Today the economy is more diversified. Geothermal energy heats nearly all homes and generates a significant share of electricity. Aluminum smelting takes advantage of cheap renewable power. Tourism has grown dramatically since the 2010 eruption of Eyjafjallajökull brought international attention to Iceland's landscapes.
