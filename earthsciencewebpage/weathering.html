Weathering, Mass Wasting & Erosion

South Africa — Earth Processes

Weathering and Mass Wasting

Chemical Weathering

The main chemical weathering process in South Africa is hydrolysis — when slightly acidic rainwater reacts with minerals in the rock and slowly breaks them down. The Cape Fold Mountains and the Highveld sit on granite and other silicate rocks packed with feldspar. Over millions of years, water has been converting that feldspar into clay. That's actually what gives the Highveld its characteristic look: all those rounded granite koppies are the result of the rock softening and wearing away from the outside in.

Carbonation is a big deal in Gauteng, Limpopo, and parts of the Northern Cape, where the ground is underlain by dolomite rock. Rainwater picks up CO2 from the air and soil and turns slightly acidic. That acid eats away at the dolomite underground, carving out hollow spaces over thousands of years. When those spaces get big enough — or when heavy rain saturates the ground above them — they can collapse without warning and open up sinkholes. Around two million people in Gauteng live on top of dolomite, which makes this one of the stranger hazards of living near Johannesburg (Council for Geoscience, n.d.-a).

Mechanical Weathering

In the Karoo, temperatures can swing from over 40°C during the day down to near freezing at night. That constant heating and cooling causes rock to expand and contract until it cracks apart. Up in the Drakensberg, freeze-thaw weathering does the heavy lifting — water seeps into joints in the cliff face, freezes, expands, and gradually wedges chunks of rock loose. Those chunks pile up as scree at the base of the slopes.

Mass Wasting

The steep parts of South Africa — the Drakensberg escarpment, the KZN coastal hills, the Cape mountains — regularly see mass wasting events. When heavy rain soaks into a hillside faster than it can drain away, the soil loses its grip and things start moving. Rockfalls, debris flows, and landslides are the most common types. Slopes that have been cleared of trees and shrubs are especially at risk, since it's the plant roots that normally keep everything in place.

Major Event — April 2022 KwaZulu-Natal Floods

In April 2022, KwaZulu-Natal got hit with more rain in a single day than many parts of the province normally see in several months — some areas recorded over 300mm in just 24 hours. The floods and landslides that followed killed at least 459 people, displaced over 40,000, and destroyed more than 12,000 homes. Hundreds of landslides swept down the coastal hills and valleys, taking houses with them, collapsing roads and bridges, and cutting communities off for days. President Ramaphosa declared a national state of disaster, and it's widely considered the worst natural disaster in South Africa's modern history (South African Government, 2022).

Scale of the Disaster: The 2022 KZN floods killed more people than any other natural disaster in South Africa since records began. eThekwini (Durban) took the worst of it — informal settlements built on steep hillsides were particularly hard hit, with debris flows burying entire streets. The N2 highway was cut in multiple places. Damage estimates exceeded R17 billion (South African Government, 2022).

Government Response

After the disaster Cabinet released R1 billion in emergency relief, and the National Disaster Management Centre coordinated the provincial response under South Africa's Disaster Management Act. The Council for Geoscience runs an ongoing Dolomite Risk Management programme — they map out which parts of Gauteng are built on unstable ground and enforce no-build buffers in the riskiest areas. There are also engineering standards (SANS 10400-B) requiring special foundations in dolomite-risk zones, and those rules have been getting more attention since the 2022 event (Council for Geoscience, n.d.-a; South African Government, 2022).

Water Erosion

South Africa sees a full range of water erosion. Gully and rill erosion are bad across the Eastern Cape, where decades of overgrazing have stripped the grass cover from communal hillsides. Sheet erosion takes topsoil off Limpopo's crop lands every summer. But the biggest impacts come from the major rivers, which shape communities and economies at a regional scale.

Orange River (Gariep River)

The Orange — also called the Gariep — is South Africa's longest river, running roughly 2,200 km from its source in the Lesotho Highlands all the way west to the Atlantic Ocean at Alexander Bay on the Namibia border. It doesn't form a proper delta, just a narrow sand-barred estuary where it meets the sea. Average flow at the mouth is around 290 m³/s, but that varies a lot because most of the lower basin is pretty dry country.

The river changes character a lot along the way. In the mountains it's steep and fast. Across the Northern Cape interior, it spreads into braided channels in the dry season when flow drops off. Then through the gorge country it cuts deep into the bedrock, including the 56-meter plunge of the Augrabies Falls. The Gariep Dam — South Africa's largest reservoir, holding about 5.34 km³ of water — and the Vanderkloof Dam keep some flow going year-round but have changed the erosion balance downstream (Department of Water and Sanitation, n.d.).

Vaal River

The Vaal starts in Mpumalanga and flows about 1,210 km west before joining the Orange near Douglas. It's the water supply for Gauteng — the Vaal Dam alone covers roughly 90% of the metro area's needs, which is over 12 million people. The upper channel meanders through the Highveld grasslands, then gets more braided and sandy heading west. The industrial zones of the Vaal Triangle (Vereeniging, Vanderbijlpark, Sasolburg) create major water quality problems on top of the usual bank erosion.

Limpopo River

The Limpopo marks South Africa's northern border with Botswana and Zimbabwe, then turns east through Mozambique to the Indian Ocean. It's about 1,750 km total from where the Crocodile and Marico rivers come together near Johannesburg to the sea. For most of the year the channel is broad, sandy, and braided — in winter it can nearly dry up. But when the rains hit, it floods badly. In 2000, Cyclone Eline pushed it over its banks in Mozambique on a catastrophic scale, killing around 800 people and displacing hundreds of thousands.

Tugela River

The Tugela starts at Mont-aux-Sources in the northern Drakensberg, about 3,282 m up, and immediately drops over the Tugela Falls — a series of cascades totaling roughly 948 m that makes it the second-highest waterfall in the world. From there it runs 502 km east to the Indian Ocean south of Richards Bay. The upper course is steep and fast through the mountains; by the time it crosses the coastal lowlands it's meandering slowly to a small estuarine mouth. Gully erosion is a serious problem on the agricultural hillsides throughout the Tugela basin.

Economic and Social Impacts

Erosion along South Africa's rivers has real costs. On the Orange, bank erosion regularly takes out irrigation infrastructure in the Northern Cape — a big deal given that the region's grape, citrus, and raisin exports depend on those systems. Higher sediment loads from farm and urban runoff push up water treatment costs for Gauteng. And flooding along the Tugela and its KZN tributaries keeps destroying roads and bridges, leaving rural communities cut off for days at a time (Department of Water and Sanitation, n.d.).

Government Mitigation

The National Water Act (1998) is the main legal framework, run by the Department of Water and Sanitation. Nine Catchment Management Agencies handle integrated water resource management across the country's major catchments. Environmental law requires a 30-meter vegetation buffer along watercourses to protect banks from development. The Working for Water programme clears invasive alien trees from river corridors — species like wattle and eucalyptus drink far more water than native plants and destabilize banks when they die. And the Gariep and Vanderkloof dams on the Orange moderate flood peaks, which has cut downstream bank erosion significantly compared to pre-dam conditions.

Wind Erosion

Wind erosion is a real problem across the dry interior — the Karoo, the Free State highveld, and the Northern Cape especially. The root cause is mostly overgrazing: over a century of intensive sheep farming stripped the protective grass and shrub cover from huge swaths of the Karoo. Without that cover, the fine silty topsoil gets picked up and blown away. The Department of Agriculture has documented widespread wind erosion across karoo farming districts, with some areas losing several centimeters of topsoil per decade.

Along the Western Cape coast, the "Cape Doctor" — a persistent southeast wind that blows through in summer — drives sand along the Atlantic shore and keeps coastal dunes on the move. It's a natural process, but it shows how powerful wind can be even in wetter parts of the country.

Karoo Dust: During droughts — which are becoming more frequent — wind strips topsoil off exposed Karoo soils and kicks up visible dust plumes big enough to track via satellite. These events are happening more often, driven by a combination of overgrazing and shifting rainfall patterns.

South Africa has signed on to the UN Convention to Combat Desertification (UNCCD) and set Land Degradation Neutrality targets, which includes tackling wind erosion in degraded areas. The Working for Land programme employs people to re-seed and rehabilitate the worst-affected patches. The Department of Agriculture pushes conservation agriculture — minimum-tillage and cover-cropping — as a way to leave crop residues on the surface to break wind and hold soil in place (Department of Forestry, Fisheries and the Environment, n.d.).

Deserts and Desertification

The Kalahari Basin — Erg Landscape

The Northern Cape's share of the Kalahari takes up a big chunk of the province. Technically, the Kalahari isn't a true desert — it gets 150–500mm of rain a year and supports a surprising amount of wildlife — but geologically it's a fossil desert: an ancient sandy basin that formed under much drier conditions tens of thousands of years ago. The old dunes are still there, now stabilized by vegetation, giving it a classic erg look — long red sand ridges, camelthorn acacias, dry riverbeds. The Kgalagadi Transfrontier Park straddles the South Africa–Botswana border and is the best place to see it, with black-maned lions, cheetah, and vast red dune horizons in every direction (South African National Parks, n.d.).

The Namib Desert — Reg and Rocky Terrain

The Namib is one of the oldest deserts on Earth, and it just barely extends into South Africa along the Atlantic coast in the far northwest — the Richtersveld area. This is mostly reg territory: flat, stony gravel plains with rugged quartzite and granite mountains. It gets under 50mm of rain a year. What keeps things alive isn't really rain — it's coastal fog rolling in off the cold Benguela Current. Succulents, gemsbok, and a few specialized insects have figured out how to live off that fog. The Richtersveld was declared a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2007, partly for its incredible diversity of succulent plants that have adapted to those conditions.

The Karoo — Semi-Arid Reg Plateau

The Great Karoo stretches across a huge sweep of the interior, between the Cape Fold Mountains and the Highveld escarpment. It gets 100–400mm of rain a year, which technically keeps it out of desert territory, but the flat stony plains covered in knee-high succulents and dwarf shrubs give it a very reg-like look. It's also one of the most botanically rich semi-arid regions in the world — the Succulent Karoo biome has over 6,000 plant species, about 40% of which grow nowhere else on Earth (SANBI, 2019).

Desertification

Desertification is a recognized and growing problem across South Africa. SANBI estimates that roughly 25–29% of the country's land surface shows signs of degradation. In the Karoo it shows up as "karoo-ification" — Karoo shrubs spreading out and taking over land that used to be grassland, pushed by overgrazing and drying trends. Around boreholes in the Kalahari and Karoo, overgrazed bare patches radiate outward, giving wind and rain erosion a foothold they wouldn't otherwise have (SANBI, 2019).

Overgrazing Legacy: The sheep-farming boom of the 19th and early 20th centuries stripped vast areas of the Karoo down to bare ground. Historical records show that land that was productive grazing country in 1850 is now nearly barren in some areas. Recovery takes decades even after grazing pressure is removed — and some of the most degraded spots may never fully recover.

South Africa's UNCCD commitments include Land Degradation Neutrality targets, and the National Veld and Forest Fire Act helps protect vegetation from uncontrolled burns. The Succulent Karoo biome gets special attention in the National Biodiversity Strategy, and rangeland management programmes help farmers set sustainable stocking rates and use rotational grazing so the land has time to recover (Department of Forestry, Fisheries and the Environment, n.d.).

Glaciers — Current and Ancient

No Current Glaciers

South Africa doesn't have any glaciers today. The country sits too far north and its mountains aren't tall enough — even the Drakensberg's highest peak tops out at around 3,408 m, which gets snow in winter but not enough cold to build real glacial ice. The sub-Antarctic Prince Edward Islands, which are South African territory, have permanent snowfields, but nothing that qualifies as an actual glacier.

Gondwana Glaciation — 300 Million Years Ago

Even so, South Africa has some of the best ancient glaciation evidence in the world. About 300–280 million years ago, South Africa was sitting near the South Pole as part of Gondwana, and a massive ice sheet covered much of what's now southern Africa — roughly comparable in scale to the modern Antarctic ice sheet.

The proof is in the Dwyka Tillite Formation — the basal layer of the Karoo rock sequence. Tillite is glacial debris that got compressed into rock over millions of years: boulders, cobbles, sand, and clay all mixed together without any sorting, exactly what you'd expect from ice rather than water or wind. The Dwyka Tillite can be hundreds of meters thick in parts of the Karoo Basin, and the boulders in it came from hundreds of kilometers away, carried by glacial flow. You can still find striated bedrock outcrops — long grooves scratched into the rock by boulders dragged under the glacier — across the Northern Cape, Eastern Cape, and Mpumalanga (Council for Geoscience, n.d.-b).

Fossil Connection: The rock layers just above the Dwyka Tillite (the Ecca Group) contain the coal seams now mined in Mpumalanga. They formed when the retreating ice sheet gave way to vast post-glacial lakes and swamps where plant matter piled up over millions of years. The ancient glaciation essentially created the conditions for South Africa's biggest energy resource.

Impact of Ancient Glaciation on South Africa Today

The Gondwana glaciation left its mark in some very practical ways. The ice sheet's weight depressed the crust and created the Karoo Basin — a massive hollow that later filled up with sedimentary layers over hundreds of millions of years. Glacial erosion also leveled much of the interior plateau, which is why the Great Karoo has that characteristic flat-to-gently-rolling look. The coal seams of Mpumalanga — which currently power most of South Africa's electricity grid — are a direct result of what grew in those post-glacial swamps. And the same rock sequence that starts with the Dwyka Tillite is also where South Africa's famous Karoo fossil record is preserved, including some of the world's best therapsid (mammal ancestor) fossils from the Permian period (Council for Geoscience, n.d.-b).
